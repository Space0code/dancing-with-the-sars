Map How it works FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Short answers. The full method is on How it works.

What am I looking at?

A map of one place with every area where the ground changed between satellite passes, drawn as coloured outlines. The picture under them is either radar, which follows the timeline, or sharp satellite imagery. That imagery has no date and can be older than a change; switch on Dated photos to see the latest cloud-free colour photo for the timeline's date instead (30 m per pixel, so blurrier). Click an outline to see what changed, when, how big, the label, and the reasons with their sources.

What do the colours mean?

Blue is Expected: a public record explains the change. Yellow is Needs review: nothing we could check explains it. Orange is High concern: it is inside a protected area, or it is large and on land where building is not allowed with nothing to explain it, or an official record shows that a required assessment or permit was not finished when the work happened. Grey means not labelled yet. The label is also written as a word on every card.

What is radar, and why not just use photos?

A radar satellite sends its own signal and measures the echo, so it works at night and through clouds. Photos need daylight and a clear sky, so a cloudy month can hide a change completely. We use both: radar for when and where, photos to look at the place.

How recent is the data?

A satellite passes a place every few days, not every day. The date under the map is the date of the picture you are looking at, and a change is dated to between two passes, never to an exact day.

How do I choose an area and a time frame?

Pick a place in the site menu, then open Changes: the Filter block there takes a time frame (From and To dates) and an area. Draw a box on the map, type its four edges (west, south, east, north), or upload a GeoJSON, zipped shapefile, KML or GPX file of up to 5 MB; the file stays on your computer. Only the changes inside are shown, counted, listed and exported, and the link in the address bar keeps your choice. The list can also be narrowed by label, kind of change and smallest size. To watch the changes appear in the order they were seen, drag the timeline or press play.

What can I download?

Export, at the top right, downloads exactly the changes you see after your time frame, area and list filters. Choose a PDF report (the map picture as you see it, the key numbers, the changes to look at first, the full table and the sources), CSV (one row per change, with a link to the source of each main reason), GeoJSON (the shapes with their labels and every reason), a zipped shapefile for GIS software, or a GeoTIFF (a raster of the labels, about 10 m pixels, one value per label; 0 means no change, and is also the file's no-data value, so GIS tools show those pixels empty). Only the PDF report carries the map picture. Every view also has a shareable link that opens the same place, date, time frame, area and change.

What is in a report, and is AI involved?

A report repeats what the cards show: the change, its label, the reasons and their sources. A short summary written by AI may be added to a report, and it is always labelled as AI-written. The facts and the labels never come from AI: they come from the satellite data and from the rules applied to public records.

Does "high concern" mean something unlawful happened?

No. It means a person should look. A change can be fully permitted and still be flagged because the records we can read do not show the permit, and a change can be a problem without being flagged. The tool does not judge anyone.

Why did it miss something, or mark something wrongly?

Pixels are 20 to 30 metres wide, so small changes are not found. Snow, frost, wet ground and wind on water can look like change. Records can be late or incomplete, so "no building on record" may only mean the registry has not caught up. Each card says how sure we are and what the satellites did and did not see.

Which places are covered?

The places in the site menu. They differ in what is connected: some have zoning, protected-area, building and solar records, others have none yet, and for those the label rests on size alone and says so.

Who made this?

A small team, for the 2026 NASA Space Apps Challenge, working with AI coding assistants. The satellite data comes from NASA, ISRO and ESA, and the public records from each place's own authorities. The code and the data format are open in the project repository.

How can I check a claim myself?

Open the change and read "Why this label?": every reason names its source and links to it. The card also shows the before and after pictures and the radar signal over time, so you can compare them with what you know of the place.
